FRIDGE OR BIN? WHAT ACTUALLY BELONGS WHERE

Food Rescue, the game on this site, asks one question over and over: is this still good, or is it finished with? Fridge if it's good, bin if it isn't.

In a game that's a clean binary. In a real kitchen it's the decision people get wrong most often, in both directions — throwing out food that was fine, and refrigerating food the fridge is actively spoiling. Here's the practical version.

THE FRIDGE IS NOT A NEUTRAL BOX

Cold slows the microbes that make food unsafe, which is why it works. But it also slows and distorts other processes, and for some foods that's a loss.

Things the fridge genuinely helps:

  • Dairy and cooked leftovers. Not optional. These are the ones where cold is doing safety work, not quality work.
  • Apples, carrots, broccoli, grapes. All keep for weeks rather than days. Apples in particular last dramatically longer cold.
  • Berries. Yes — but don't wash them until you're about to eat them. Surface moisture is what turns a punnet of strawberries to mush.
  • Leafy greens and herbs, loosely wrapped so they don't sweat.

Things the fridge quietly ruins:

  • Tomatoes. Cold suppresses the aroma compounds that make a tomato taste of anything, and the texture goes mealy. A fridge tomato is safe and boring. Room temperature, out of the bag.
  • Bananas. Chilling damages the peel — it blackens fast — and unripe ones stop ripening properly. Keep them out, and away from other fruit, because they give off enough ethylene to hurry everything nearby along.
  • Bread. Counterintuitive but well established: bread goes stale fastest at fridge temperature, faster than on the counter. Freeze it or leave it out; the fridge is the worst of the three.
  • Onions and garlic. Cold and damp makes them soften and sprout. Dark, dry, airy.

Worth admitting: the game puts a tomato in the fridge, because the tomato is standing in for "still good" and the fridge is standing in for "keep it". The simplification is deliberate. The nuance is this article.

DATE LABELS SAY TWO DIFFERENT THINGS

Most avoidable household food waste comes down to one confusion, and it's a confusion the labels themselves create by looking identical.

  • Use by is a safety date. It appears on things where the risk is invisible — fresh meat, fish, ready meals, some dairy. Past it, don't, and don't rely on smell, because the organisms that matter here don't announce themselves.
  • Best before is a quality date. Biscuits, pasta, tinned goods, most dry store-cupboard items. It's the manufacturer's estimate of when it stops being at its best, not a cliff edge. Plenty of it is fine well past the date, and your senses are a reasonable guide.

Treating a best-before as a use-by is how perfectly good food ends up in the bin. Treating a use-by as a best-before is the mistake that can actually hurt you. They are not interchangeable and it's a shame they look the same.

WHAT'S GENUINELY FINISHED WITH

The bin items in the game — a banana peel, a chicken bone, a wrapper, an empty cup, an empty box — are all obvious. Real waste mostly isn't obvious, it's just forgotten. A bag of salad at the back of a shelf, half a jar of something, leftovers nobody claimed.

Which points at the only tactic that reliably helps: the problem is visibility, not knowledge. Two things that work:

  • One shelf for "eat this next". Anything opened or nearly out of time goes there, at eye level. Nothing gets forgotten at the front.
  • Shop for four days, not seven. Most household waste is food bought in good faith for a week that didn't survive one.

Peels, cores and bones aren't rubbish everywhere, either. Where food waste is collected separately they're a stream of their own, and bones in particular don't belong in a home compost heap — they don't break down and they attract animals. Local rules vary enough that it's worth checking yours once.

THE SCALE OF IT

Roughly a third of the food produced in the world is lost or wasted, and households — not shops or restaurants — are the single largest slice of that at the consumer end. It's an unusual environmental problem in that the fix is entirely within reach of an individual and costs nothing. Mostly it's just noticing.

HOW TO GET GOOD AT FOOD RESCUE

Food Rescue looks like Snake with an errand attached, and for the first minute it plays like it. Then your body gets long, the two drop-off zones stop being destinations and start being obstacles, and it turns into a different game.

Here's what's actually going on underneath, and the handful of things that make the difference.

THE ONE RULE MOST PLAYERS MISS

While you're carrying something, the wrong corner is a hazard.

Touching either zone while carrying triggers a drop-off. Not just the right one — either. So if you've picked up an apple, the bin in the top-right is no longer neutral scenery you can drive through. Clip it and the apple goes in the bin, you forfeit the 25 points, and the game stops to explain why.

That single fact reshapes every route. Empty-handed, both zones are free space and you can cut straight through them — eight cells of guaranteed-clear board in each top corner, which is useful when you're long. Carrying, one of them is a mine. Whether the top-left is a target or a trap depends entirely on what you happen to be holding.

THE GAME TELLS YOU THE ANSWER — READ IT

The moment you pick something up, the correct destination gets a dashed green outline. You don't have to decide anything under pressure; you have to look.

Players lose points here not by getting the sorting wrong but by committing to a direction before glancing at the outline. Pick up, look at which corner is ringed, then steer. It costs you one step.

GROWTH IS PER DELIVERY, NOT PER PICKUP

You grow one segment when you drop something off — and that includes dropping it in the wrong place. Picking food up costs you nothing in length.

So a wrong delivery is worse than it feels. You don't lose points you already had, but you forfeit the 25, and you still take the segment and still take the speed increase. It's a full-price delivery with no payout. The pause makes it feel like a gentle correction; it isn't.

THERE IS A SPEED CEILING, AND YOU'LL REACH IT

Each drop-off shortens the step interval by 3ms, starting at 165ms and stopping at 95ms. That's about 24 drop-offs to maximum speed.

This is the most useful thing to know about the difficulty curve. After roughly two dozen deliveries the game is as fast as it will ever be, and everything that gets harder from there is your own length. If you can survive the transition around delivery 20–25, the reflex demand flattens out and the game becomes pure route planning. Most runs end in that window, from players expecting it to keep accelerating and panicking accordingly.

STAY OFF THE TOP ROW

Both zones sit on the top two rows, in opposite corners. That makes the top of the board the busiest area and the most dangerous, because the top edge is a wall and a long body along it leaves you nowhere to turn.

Approach a corner from below, along a column, and leave the same way. Don't run the top row from one corner to the other — that's the longest possible exposure to a wall, in the one region you'll keep needing to revisit.

Park low. Food spawns anywhere except the two zones and your own body, so the average item is nearer the middle-bottom than the corners. Waiting in the lower half puts you closer to most spawns and further from the walls that kill you.

YOU CAN CHASE YOUR OWN TAIL

The last cell of your body moves out of the way on the same step you'd hit it, so running directly into your own tail tip is not a crash.

This makes tight loops survivable and is the main tool for stalling when you're long and the food is somewhere awkward. Circle your own body rather than committing to a route you haven't worked out. It's also the difference between a board that looks full and a board that actually is.

THE ROUTE THAT WORKS

  1. Wait in the lower middle when you're empty-handed. Most spawns come to you.
  2. Collect, then look at which corner has the dashed ring.
  3. Climb the column nearest that corner, not the top row.
  4. Drop off and drop straight back down the same way. Don't linger up there.
  5. When long, cut through the empty zone — the one you aren't carrying for is free space until you pick something up.

ON THE SORTING ITSELF

None of the items are ambiguous. Peels, bones, wrappers and empty containers go in the bin; whole food goes in the fridge. Nothing appears in both lists, so a picture always means one thing.

Which is a simplification, and a deliberate one — a real kitchen has tomatoes and bananas and bread, none of which actually want to be in a fridge. The game is teaching still-good versus finished-with, not fridge logistics.
